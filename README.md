# Memory, History, and Becoming:
## Gabriel García Márquez's *One Hundred Years of Solitude* and Octavio Paz's *Piedra de Sol*

Gabriel García Márquez's *One Hundred Years of Solitude* (1967) and Octavio Paz's
*Piedra de Sol* (*Sunstone*, 1957) are very different works: one is a multigenerational
Colombian novel associated with magical realism, while the other is a major Mexican
modernist poem.

Nevertheless, the two works occupy overlapping Latin American intellectual territory.

Both explore:

- memory and forgetting,
- cyclical rather than simply linear time,
- the persistence of the past within the present,
- personal experience as a gateway into collective history,
- violence and political power,
- the limitations of modernity and material progress,
- love and human connection,
- and the possibility—or failure—of escaping historical repetition.

The recent Netflix adaptation of *One Hundred Years of Solitude* makes many of these
otherwise abstract ideas unusually visible. Macondo becomes a physical world in which
viewers can watch memory, modernization, political authority, family identity, and
historical repetition gradually transform a community.

---

# Two Major Latin American Writers

## Octavio Paz

**Octavio Paz (1914–1998)** was a Mexican poet, essayist, diplomat, and intellectual who
received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1990.

His writing repeatedly examined questions involving:

- Mexican and Latin American identity,
- modernity,
- history,
- solitude,
- love,
- language,
- myth,
- memory,
- politics,
- and the relationship between individual consciousness and civilization.

His 1957 poem *Piedra de Sol* is particularly concerned with time, memory, love, history,
and human consciousness.

The poem itself has a circular structure. Its ending returns to its beginning, reinforcing
the idea that time does not operate merely as:

**Past → Present → Future**

Instead, past and present continually interact.

---

## Gabriel García Márquez

**Gabriel García Márquez (1927–2014)** was a Colombian novelist and journalist who
received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1982.

His *One Hundred Years of Solitude* follows several generations of the Buendía family
and the development of the fictional town of Macondo.

Macondo begins as an isolated settlement but gradually encounters:

- scientific and technological innovations,
- government authority,
- political ideology,
- civil war,
- economic modernization,
- foreign corporate power,
- social upheaval,
- violence,
- and historical forgetting.

The history of one family consequently becomes intertwined with the history of a
community and, more broadly, with recurring patterns in Colombian and Latin American
history.

---

# The Central Connection: The Past Remains Present

One of the strongest connections between *Piedra de Sol* and *One Hundred Years of
Solitude* is their rejection of a simple conception of time in which the past disappears
once the present arrives.

In his 2014 essay **"Love, Memory and Being in Octavio Paz's Piedra de Sol,"**
Fresno State scholar Saúl Jiménez-Sandoval interprets Paz partly through philosopher
Henri Bergson.

Jiménez-Sandoval argues that memory connects:

> "the lessons of the past (personal and historical) and a consciousness of the present."

He consequently interprets *Piedra de Sol* as demonstrating the **survival of the
consciousness of the past in the present**.

The past and present therefore do not exist as completely separated compartments.

Instead:

**Past ↔ Present**

and this interaction creates the possibilities of:

**Future / Becoming**

This provides an especially useful framework for understanding García Márquez.

---

# *Piedra de Sol*: Remembering Makes Becoming Possible

In Jiménez-Sandoval's interpretation of Paz, memory is not merely nostalgia.

Memory provides **knowledge necessary for action**.

The individual remembers personal experience.

Personal memory expands into historical consciousness.

Historical consciousness allows the individual to recognize patterns of injustice.

Recognition creates the possibility of choosing a different response.

The process can therefore be represented as:

**Past**

↓

**Memory**

↓

**Historical Consciousness**

↓

**Recognition**

↓

**Choice**

↓

**Action**

↓

**Becoming**

Jiménez-Sandoval emphasizes that human beings possess the capacity for voluntary action.
The reflective individual can interpret humanity's past and decide how to respond to the
present.

Memory therefore creates a degree of freedom.

---

# *One Hundred Years of Solitude*: Forgetting Produces Repetition

García Márquez frequently explores almost the opposite condition.

Macondo repeatedly suffers from failures of memory.

The most obvious early example is the **insomnia plague**.

Rebeca introduces the mysterious illness into the Buendía household. People initially
lose the ability to sleep.

But insomnia gradually produces something much more dangerous:

**Insomnia**

↓

**Memory Loss**

↓

**Loss of Names**

↓

**Loss of Meaning**

↓

**Loss of Historical Identity**

The inhabitants eventually begin labeling ordinary objects because they fear forgetting
what things are and what they are used for.

This apparently whimsical magical-realist episode introduces one of the central problems
of the entire story:

> **What happens to a society that loses its ability to remember?**

---

# Paz and García Márquez as Mirror Images

Seen comparatively, the two works can almost be placed opposite one another.

| *Piedra de Sol* / Paz | *One Hundred Years of Solitude* / García Márquez |
|---|---|
| Memory | Forgetting |
| Historical consciousness | Historical amnesia |
| Recognition | Failure to recognize |
| Voluntary action | Repetition |
| Becoming | Recurrence |
| Connection | Solitude |
| Possibility | Entrapment |
| Past informs present | Forgotten past returns |
| Consciousness may produce change | Amnesia permits repetition |

The comparison should not be treated as an exact opposition. Both works are considerably
more complicated.

But the contrast provides a powerful interpretive framework:

> **For Paz, remembering can create the possibility of becoming.  
> For García Márquez, forgetting can condemn people to repetition.**

---

# Cyclical Time

Both works challenge the Western-modern assumption that history necessarily moves
forward through continuous progress.

A conventional progressive model of history looks something like:

**Past → Development → Modernization → Progress → Better Future**

Neither Paz nor García Márquez is comfortable with that simple progression.

## Paz

*Piedra de Sol* is structurally circular.

Its opening verses return at the conclusion.

Time advances, doubles back, circles around, and returns.

Jiménez-Sandoval emphasizes precisely this feature in his analysis, observing that the
poem's river:

**moves forward → returns → circles → arrives again**

The past therefore accompanies the present rather than simply disappearing behind it.

## García Márquez

García Márquez creates a similar effect through generations.

The Buendía family repeatedly uses the names:

**José Arcadio**

and

**Aureliano**

The repetition becomes more than a genealogical curiosity.

Similar personalities, desires, mistakes, conflicts, and forms of solitude recur across
generations.

The reader gradually develops the unsettling feeling:

> **We have been here before.**

That feeling is fundamental to the novel.

---

# Family History Becomes Human History

Another important connection concerns scale.

Both writers begin with intimate human experience and expand outward toward history.

Jiménez-Sandoval describes this explicitly in his interpretation of Paz:

> "the poet's personal history is the basis for an understanding of humanity's history."

In *Piedra de Sol*, personal memory and erotic love gradually open into reflection upon
historical violence, political institutions, war, injustice, and humanity itself.

García Márquez performs a comparable expansion through the Buendía family.

At first, *One Hundred Years of Solitude* appears to concern:

**a marriage → children → relatives → a household**

But the family becomes intertwined with:

**Macondo → Colombia → Latin American history → recurring human experience**

The domestic and historical become inseparable.

---

# Modernity Is Not Automatically Progress

Both writers are also skeptical of the assumption that technological or economic
development automatically produces human advancement.

This becomes particularly visible in *One Hundred Years of Solitude*.

Macondo encounters extraordinary innovations.

The gypsies bring:

- magnets,
- scientific instruments,
- alchemy,
- technological curiosities,
- and eventually the astonishing experience of ice.

José Arcadio Buendía responds with tremendous enthusiasm.

Knowledge appears capable of transforming the world.

But technological development does not automatically produce wisdom.

Macondo eventually acquires increasing connections with commerce, political institutions,
transportation, warfare, and international capitalism.

Modernization creates possibilities.

It also creates new forms of domination.

---

# Paz's Critique of Material Modernity

Jiménez-Sandoval finds a comparable critique in *Piedra de Sol*.

He argues that Paz contrasts genuine human consciousness with a modern materialistic
system associated with:

- capitalism,
- hierarchy,
- consumption,
- war,
- institutional masks,
- and the reduction of human beings to economic functions.

Historical consciousness allows the poetic voice to look behind the respectable
appearances of institutions and recognize contradictions between their public identities
and their actual behavior.

Jiménez-Sandoval describes this consciousness as capable of shattering:

> "the masks, titles, laws and pretensions"

that support hierarchical systems of material values.

Thus both Paz and García Márquez ask an important Latin American question:

> **What does "progress" mean if material modernization does not produce greater human
> dignity, freedom, memory, or justice?**

---

# Narrative and Power

This leads to another important connection.

Power is not exercised exclusively through:

**weapons + technology + money + government**

Power also operates through:

**memory + symbols + language + historical narrative**

Who gets remembered?

Who gets forgotten?

Which events become heroic?

Which become embarrassing?

Which disappear?

Who gets to explain what happened?

These questions become especially important in *One Hundred Years of Solitude*.

As Macondo becomes incorporated into larger political and economic systems, conflicts
increasingly arise not merely over **what happened**, but over **what society will
remember happened**.

Historical memory itself becomes contested territory.

---

# Writing Becomes Resistance to Forgetting

This gives the insomnia plague an additional meaning.

When Macondo begins forgetting, its inhabitants begin **writing things down**.

Objects receive labels.

Language becomes an external memory system.

That creates an intriguing meta-literary interpretation:

**The people of Macondo write because they are afraid of forgetting.**

And García Márquez writes *One Hundred Years of Solitude*.

The novel itself can therefore be understood as an enormous act of cultural memory.

Literature preserves experiences that conventional political history may overlook:

- family memories,
- workers,
- ordinary people,
- myths,
- indigenous and folk traditions,
- political violence,
- private suffering,
- cultural contradictions,
- and alternative interpretations of history.

Literature becomes one mechanism through which a civilization remembers itself.

---

# Love and Solitude

There is another important relationship between Paz and García Márquez that should not
be lost beneath the political interpretation.

**Paz emphasizes love.**

**García Márquez emphasizes solitude.**

For Paz, as interpreted by Jiménez-Sandoval, genuine love can break down the boundaries
between individuals.

Jiménez-Sandoval ultimately describes Paz's poetry as providing:

> "a route to the actualization of the self through love"

that makes possible:

> "social integration, responsibility, and justice."

Love moves the individual beyond isolation.

García Márquez repeatedly examines what happens when human beings **fail to establish
lasting connections with one another**.

The Buendías love intensely, desire intensely, fight intensely, and pursue extraordinary
ambitions.

Yet they repeatedly fall into forms of solitude.

Thus another useful comparison emerges:

**Paz: Love → Communion**

**García Márquez: Solitude → Separation**

Both are asking how individuals escape the boundaries of the isolated self.

---

# History and Repetition

Jiménez-Sandoval makes a particularly important statement near the conclusion of his
analysis of Paz.

He argues that historical crimes remain with humanity:

> "haunting and hurting us."

Unless people become conscious of those crimes, he warns:

> "they will keep repeating and preventing our true becoming."

This observation provides an extraordinary bridge to *One Hundred Years of Solitude*.

García Márquez creates a fictional world in which **repetition becomes visible**.

Names repeat.

Personalities repeat.

Political conflicts repeat.

Family mistakes repeat.

Violence repeats.

The circumstances change, but recognizable patterns return.

The problem therefore becomes:

> **How can human beings escape historical cycles they cannot recognize because they
> have forgotten their own history?**

---

# A Shared Latin American Problem

Both Paz and García Márquez write from societies shaped by extraordinarily complicated
historical layers:

**Indigenous civilizations**

↓

**Spanish conquest**

↓

**Colonial society**

↓

**Catholic cultural influence**

↓

**Independence**

↓

**Nation-building**

↓

**Civil conflict and revolution**

↓

**European and U.S. economic influence**

↓

**Modernization**

↓

**Contemporary Latin American identity**

No single layer completely eliminates the previous one.

The past continually survives inside the present.

Consequently, Latin American identity cannot simply be understood as either:

**Indigenous**

or

**European**

or

**modern Western**

or

**postcolonial**

It is constructed through the continuing interaction of all these histories.

Both Paz and García Márquez turn that complicated historical layering into literature.

---

# Magical Realism and Poetic Consciousness

Their literary techniques are different but perform related work.

## García Márquez: Magical Realism

García Márquez allows:

**history + myth + folklore + religion + supernatural events + ordinary life**

to inhabit the same narrative world.

The extraordinary is frequently described as ordinary.

The supposedly rational world can appear absurd.

This destabilizes the assumption that modern Western rationalism possesses the only
legitimate way of describing reality.

## Paz: Poetic Consciousness

Paz similarly destabilizes ordinary categories.

Past and present coexist.

Individual and humanity overlap.

Love becomes simultaneously:

- physical,
- psychological,
- political,
- historical,
- spiritual,
- and metaphysical.

Ordinary chronological time temporarily dissolves.

Thus both writers create literary spaces where conventional boundaries become unstable.

---

# The Netflix Series as an Entry Point

The Netflix adaptation of *One Hundred Years of Solitude* makes these themes particularly
accessible because ideas that can seem abstract on the page become visually concrete.

The viewer sees:

**Macondo being created.**

Then:

**outsiders arrive.**

Then:

**technology arrives.**

Then:

**disease threatens memory.**

Then:

**government arrives.**

Then:

**political identity begins entering everyday life.**

The apparently minor dispute over painting houses blue becomes significant because
political power has reached a community that previously understood itself largely through
its own internal traditions.

Macondo is gradually incorporated into a much larger historical system.

The series can therefore be watched simultaneously on three levels:

**The Buendía Family**

↓

**The History of Macondo**

↓

**Colombian / Latin American Historical Experience**

---

# A Combined Paz–García Márquez Framework

Putting the two writers together produces a useful model:

**EXPERIENCE**

↓

**MEMORY**

↓

**NARRATIVE**

↓

**IDENTITY**

↓

**PERCEPTION OF THE PRESENT**

↓

**CHOICE**

↓

**ACTION**

↓

**FUTURE HISTORY**

↓

**NEW MEMORY**

The cycle then begins again.

The crucial question becomes whether memory remains sufficiently alive for people to
recognize what they are repeating.

---

# Remembering Does Not Mean Being Imprisoned by the Past

Neither comparison requires the conclusion that people must simply accept whatever
identity history gives them.

Quite the opposite.

Jiménez-Sandoval's interpretation of Paz suggests that knowledge of the past can make
**voluntary action** possible.

The past informs the present without completely determining the future.

Thus:

> **Memory does not have to imprison us within history.  
> Memory can give us the consciousness necessary to choose what happens next.**

García Márquez shows the darker counterpart:

> **When memory disappears, people may unknowingly reproduce the very histories from
> which they believe themselves free.**

---

# Central Comparative Takeaway

The relationship among Octavio Paz, Gabriel García Márquez, *Piedra de Sol*, and
*One Hundred Years of Solitude* can therefore be summarized around one fundamental
problem:

> **How does a human being—or an entire culture—remain conscious of the past without
> becoming imprisoned by it?**

Paz's answer, particularly through Jiménez-Sandoval's interpretation, emphasizes:

**memory → consciousness → love → ethical action → becoming**

García Márquez explores what happens when that process breaks:

**forgetting → loss of meaning → solitude → repetition**

Together they suggest a powerful understanding of history:

> **The past continues to exercise power in the present, but historical consciousness
> gives human beings some ability to determine what that past will mean for the future.**

In that sense, *Piedra de Sol* and *One Hundred Years of Solitude* can be read as
complementary explorations of one of the central problems of modern Latin American
thought:

**how to remember enough of the past to become something different in the future.**

# Memory, Moral Reckoning, and Institutional Leadership:  
## Reading President Saúl Jiménez-Sandoval's César Chávez Response Through His Earlier Scholarship

Fresno State President **Saúl Jiménez-Sandoval's response to the allegations concerning César Chávez** can be understood as more than an administrative reaction to a difficult public controversy. Read alongside Jiménez-Sandoval's earlier literary scholarship—particularly his 2014 essay **"Love, Memory and Being in Octavio Paz's *Piedra de Sol*"**—the response illustrates a recognizable intellectual approach to the relationship among **history, memory, identity, ethical judgment, and action**.

This does not establish that Jiménez-Sandoval consciously applied his earlier literary scholarship when composing the university statement. Nor should his analysis of Octavio Paz simply be treated as a blueprint for his presidency. Nevertheless, the similarities are striking enough to illuminate how a scholar trained to think deeply about **memory and historical consciousness** might approach an institutional crisis involving the meaning of a historical figure.

---

## The Crisis Is Not Simply About a Statue

The immediate problem facing Fresno State concerns César Chávez and the serious allegations that have emerged about his conduct. But institutionally, the problem is considerably larger.

The Chávez statue in Fresno State's Peace Garden is not merely an object depicting a historical individual. A monument is an act of **institutional memory**. By placing a person permanently within a prominent public space, an institution communicates something like:

> **This person represents something about our history that we believe deserves to be remembered and honored.**

Consequently, disturbing new information concerning that individual creates a conflict between **received historical memory and present ethical consciousness**.

Jiménez-Sandoval acknowledges that conflict directly. He writes that the allegations require:

> **"our full attention and moral reckoning"**

and announces that the statue will first be covered while the university determines the appropriate steps toward its removal.

But he does something equally significant immediately afterward: **he refuses to allow reconsideration of Chávez to become repudiation of the farmworker movement itself.**

He reminds the campus that the monument was originally erected:

> **"to honor the spirit of peaceful assembly and the broader labor movement that has shaped this region."**

The distinction is crucial.

The emerging institutional narrative becomes:

**César Chávez is subject to moral reconsideration.**

But:

**The struggles, sacrifices, and achievements of Central Valley farmworkers remain worthy of historical recognition.**

Rather than simply destroying an old narrative and substituting a new one, Jiménez-Sandoval attempts to **reconstruct institutional memory around a broader and more durable historical subject**.

---

## An Earlier Intellectual Framework: Memory Makes Ethical Action Possible

This approach bears a notable resemblance to Jiménez-Sandoval's academic work more than a decade earlier.

In his chapter **"Love, Memory and Being in Octavio Paz's *Piedra de Sol*"**, published in *The Willow and the Spiral: Essays on Octavio Paz and the Poetic Imagination* (2014), Jiménez-Sandoval challenges interpretations that place love and history in irreconcilable opposition.

Instead, he argues:

> **"it is the consciousness of history that makes possible a conception of the nature of love in Paz's poem."**

Even more significantly, he proposes that an individual's personal history can provide a basis for understanding humanity's larger history. [Jiménez-Sandoval, "Love, Memory and Being," p. 190.]

Drawing upon Henri Bergson's *Matter and Memory*, Jiménez-Sandoval develops an understanding of memory in which the past is not simply a collection of events that have disappeared.

The past remains **active within the present**.

Memory allows the individual to bring the experience of the past into present consciousness so that the present can be understood differently—and so that future action can be chosen differently.

As Jiménez-Sandoval explains, memory establishes a relationship between:

> **"the lessons of the past (personal and historical) and a consciousness of the present."**

[Jiménez-Sandoval, p. 191.]

This produces a powerful sequence:

**Past → Memory → Consciousness → Ethical Judgment → Action → Future**

The past therefore matters not because people should remain trapped within it, but because **understanding the past expands the possibilities for voluntary action in the present**.

---

## The Past Is Not Finished

One of the most important propositions in Jiménez-Sandoval's essay is that past and present should not be understood as completely separate chronological territories.

Discussing Paz through Bergson, he argues that:

> **"the past and the present do not represent two successive moments"**

and that the past continues to be manifested within the present that carries it forward.

[Jiménez-Sandoval, p. 194.]

That idea appears remarkably compatible with the language of the Fresno State statement.

Jiménez-Sandoval writes of the Central Valley:

> **"The legacy of those struggles continues to shape our identity."**

That sentence could almost serve as a practical institutional expression of the philosophical argument developed in his earlier scholarship.

The farmworker movement isn't treated merely as something that **happened**.

It continues to participate in determining **who the community understands itself to be**.

History becomes memory.

Memory becomes identity.

Identity informs present action.

---

## New Knowledge Requires Reconsideration of an Existing Narrative

Jiménez-Sandoval's earlier scholarship also rejects the idea that remembering the past means mechanically preserving whatever interpretation of the past has previously become established.

Memory should instead make critical reconsideration possible.

In his discussion of *Piedra de Sol*, historical consciousness allows the poetic voice to recognize the injustices of the historical past and thereby challenge the present's distorted understanding of those events.

Jiménez-Sandoval writes that the poetic consciousness:

> **"corrects the present's distorted vision of past lessons."**

[Jiménez-Sandoval, p. 191.]

This provides an especially useful framework for understanding the Chávez controversy.

The historical event represented by the Peace Garden monument has not changed. The farmworker movement occurred. Chávez played an important historical role within it. The statue was erected in 1996.

What has changed is **the information available to the present**.

That new information requires the university to ask whether the symbolic interpretation embodied by the monument remains ethically sustainable.

Thus:

**New knowledge  
→ changes present consciousness  
→ which changes interpretation of the past  
→ which changes appropriate action in the present.**

Covering and potentially removing the statue consequently need not represent historical amnesia.

Within this framework, it can represent precisely the opposite:

**historical memory being actively reconsidered.**

---

## Separating a Historical Movement From One of Its Symbols

Perhaps the most consequential narrative decision in Jiménez-Sandoval's statement is his separation of **César Chávez the individual** from **Central Valley farmworkers as a historical community**.

He writes:

> **"we must now honor that history by acknowledging the harm done to the victims while we continue to highlight the immense value of the farmworkers whose hard work and sacrifice continue to feed our nation."**

This is a sophisticated act of historical reframing.

The university does not have to choose between two simplistic alternatives:

**Preserve Chávez → preserve farmworker history**

or

**Reject Chávez → reject farmworker history.**

Instead, Jiménez-Sandoval constructs a third possibility:

**Reconsider Chávez  
while preserving the farmworker movement  
and incorporating the alleged victims into the community's historical memory.**

In effect, the historical narrative becomes **larger rather than smaller**.

---

## Memory Can Expose the Masks of Power

This becomes even more interesting in light of the explicitly political dimension of Jiménez-Sandoval's interpretation of Paz.

In discussing *Piedra de Sol*, he argues that historical and personal consciousness allows the poetic voice to recognize the false appearances maintained by powerful institutions.

He describes how this consciousness can:

> **"shatter the masks, titles, laws and pretensions that force human beings into a symbolic order"**

and thereby reveal contradictions between the respectable appearances of power and the actual behavior of those who possess it.

[Jiménez-Sandoval, p. 203.]

That argument is particularly relevant to monuments.

A monument necessarily simplifies.

It transforms a complicated human being into a **public symbol**.

The individual becomes:

**leader  
hero  
reformer  
founder  
liberator.**

The symbolic identity can eventually become so powerful that it obscures the historical person's complexity.

Jiménez-Sandoval's earlier scholarship suggests that historical consciousness should do precisely the opposite: it should allow individuals and communities to **look behind inherited symbolic identities when new evidence demands reconsideration**.

---

## The Alternative to Memory Is Repetition

The strongest connection may appear near the conclusion of Jiménez-Sandoval's essay.

Reflecting upon Paz's invocation of historical victims and recurring human violence, Jiménez-Sandoval argues that the crimes of history remain present because they continue to affect humanity.

He writes:

> **"these crimes are still with us, haunting and hurting us."**

And then comes the crucial warning:

> **"Lest we say something, lest we become conscious of them, they will keep repeating and preventing our true becoming."**

[Jiménez-Sandoval, p. 207.]

This makes memory an ethical obligation.

Remembering isn't simply preserving heritage.

It is one of the mechanisms through which a society attempts to **avoid reproducing historical injustice**.

That reasoning gives additional significance to the language of the presidential statement:

> **"We will take the time to listen, to learn, and to act in a way that honors our history and respects our ongoing responsibility to one another."**

The verbs are revealing:

**listen → learn → act**

Compare them with the intellectual progression of the earlier essay:

**remember → become conscious → reflect → choose → act**

The correspondence should not be overstated, but the intellectual resemblance is difficult to miss.

---

## From Literary Scholar to Institutional Leader

Jiménez-Sandoval concludes his *Piedra de Sol* essay by emphasizing voluntary human action.

A reflective human being is not condemned simply to reproduce inherited responses. Instead:

> **"human beings have the power to choose the response necessary before a specific stimulus."**

He then makes the ethical implication explicit:

> **"Truly voluntary action on the part of a reflective being, therefore, interprets and assesses humanity's past in light of the ethical concept of love and its relationship to justice."**

[Jiménez-Sandoval, p. 209.]

His final formulation connects poetry and historical consciousness to:

> **"social integration, responsibility, and justice."**

[Jiménez-Sandoval, p. 209.]

More than a decade later, President Jiménez-Sandoval confronts an institutional problem involving precisely these elements:

- historical memory,
- newly perceived injustice,
- an established cultural symbol,
- community identity,
- responsibility,
- and the necessity of institutional action.

His response does not simply say:

**The old narrative was false; erase it.**

Nor does it say:

**The old narrative is useful; defend it.**

Instead, it follows a more complicated pattern:

**Remember the history.  
Accept the new information.  
Reconsider the symbol.  
Acknowledge those who were harmed.  
Preserve what remains valuable in the larger historical tradition.  
Then act.**

---

## Narrative, Memory, and Leadership

This suggests an important distinction between **narrative management** and **historical consciousness**.

A leader concerned merely with narrative management asks:

> **What story should we tell that best protects the institution?**

The intellectual framework Jiménez-Sandoval developed in his scholarship points toward a more demanding question:

> **What does a fuller consciousness of our past require us to become in the present?**

That difference matters.

Narrative certainly remains a form of institutional power. Presidents, universities, governments, museums, monuments, curricula, and public ceremonies all participate in deciding **what a community remembers and what those memories mean**.

But Jiménez-Sandoval's earlier scholarship suggests that legitimate narrative agency cannot simply mean inventing whatever version of history presently serves one's interests.

Memory must remain capable of **challenging the narrative itself**.

The past must sometimes disturb the present.

And when it does, the reflective community possesses the ability—and perhaps the responsibility—to choose a different future.

---

## A Scholar's Reasoning Expressed Through Leadership

It would be too strong to claim that Jiménez-Sandoval's 2014 interpretation of Octavio Paz directly caused his response to the Chávez controversy. Establishing such a connection would require his own testimony or additional evidence.

What can reasonably be observed, however, is a notable **continuity of reasoning**.

In his scholarship, Jiménez-Sandoval describes:

**memory as the survival of the past within the present;  
historical consciousness as a means of exposing injustice;  
reflection as an interruption of automatic responses;  
ethical consciousness as a basis for voluntary action;  
and action as the means through which a different future becomes possible.**

In his response as Fresno State president, we can see a remarkably similar progression.

The inherited monument represents one understanding of the past.

New information disrupts that understanding.

The university is called upon to remember more completely.

Historical memory must be reconsidered through present ethical consciousness.

The institution then acts—not by abandoning the Central Valley's farmworker history, but by attempting to **reconstruct its commemoration of that history around a broader conception of dignity and justice**.

Perhaps the most concise way to characterize the continuity between Jiménez-Sandoval the scholar and Jiménez-Sandoval the president is therefore:

> **The past should neither imprison the present nor be discarded by it. The past must remain available to consciousness so that individuals and communities can reconsider what it means, recognize injustice, and exercise their freedom to become something different.**

That principle sits near the center of his interpretation of Octavio Paz.

It also provides a compelling intellectual lens through which to understand his response to one of the more difficult problems of institutional memory a university president can face.

# A German Connection to *One Thousand and One Nights* in the Context of Rommel's North African Campaign

At first glance, **Erwin Rommel's North African campaign** and **One Thousand and One Nights** appear unrelated. One belongs to twentieth-century military history, the other to medieval literature. However, they intersect through Germany's long-standing scholarly and cultural interest in the Middle East.

## Germany's Long Fascination with the Orient

Long before World War II, German scholars, writers, and artists were deeply interested in the cultures of the Middle East.

Notable examples include:

- **Johann Wolfgang von Goethe's** *West–Eastern Divan* (1819), inspired by Persian poetry.
- German translations of *One Thousand and One Nights* throughout the 18th and 19th centuries.
- German universities becoming leading centers for the study of Arabic, Persian, and Islamic history.
- Archaeological expeditions to Mesopotamia, Egypt, and the Levant.

By the early twentieth century, educated Germans would likely have recognized stories such as **Scheherazade**, **Sindbad**, and **Ali Baba**, much as educated Britons or French readers would.

---

## Rommel's North African Theater

When Rommel arrived in Libya in 1941, he entered a landscape that had long captured the European imagination:

- caravan routes
- desert fortresses
- ancient Roman ruins
- Arab and Berber societies
- cities such as Tripoli and Benghazi

These places were geographically connected to the broader world that had inspired many stories associated with *One Thousand and One Nights*, even though the tales themselves originated across a much wider region stretching from Persia and Iraq to Egypt and Syria.

---

## Military Reality vs. Literary Imagination

It is important to distinguish between the literary world of *One Thousand and One Nights* and the historical reality of Rommel's campaign.

The literary world features:

- merchants
- storytellers
- caliphs
- bustling marketplaces
- magical adventures

Rommel's campaign involved:

- armored divisions
- mechanized warfare
- aircraft
- logistics
- fuel shortages
- modern military strategy

The connection is therefore **cultural rather than operational**.

---

## A Shared European Imagination

For many Europeans during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, North Africa and the Middle East occupied a place in the imagination shaped by:

- biblical history
- classical antiquity
- Orientalist art
- travel literature
- *One Thousand and One Nights*

As a result, German soldiers serving in North Africa—including Rommel's troops—were operating in a region that many back home already associated with centuries of stories and historical fascination.

---

## A Historical Perspective

Rather than seeing Rommel's campaign as connected to *One Thousand and One Nights* directly, it is more accurate to view both as part of Germany's broader historical engagement with the Middle East:

- **Literary engagement** through translations, scholarship, and appreciation of Persian and Arabic classics.
- **Academic engagement** through Oriental studies, archaeology, and language scholarship.
- **Military engagement** during World War II in North Africa.

These represent different forms of interaction with the same broad region across different centuries.
